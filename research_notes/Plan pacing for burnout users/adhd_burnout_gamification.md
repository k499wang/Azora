# ADHD, Burnout/Depression and Gamification: Engagement Evidence for Plan Pacing

Evidence grades used below: **[MA]** meta-analysis / systematic review, **[RCT]** randomized trial, **[Pilot/Open]** non-randomized or small pilot, **[Obs]** observational / usage-panel data, **[Qual]** qualitative / autoethnographic, **[Opinion]** practitioner or press.

## ADHD and digital interventions: engagement, reward sensitivity/delay aversion, and routines as structure

### Takeaway
App evidence for adult ADHD is thin and low quality. Retention in open studies falls roughly 60% by week 7, and incentives raise logging without improving real adherence. The strongest evidence for adult ADHD supports structured, skills-based routines delivered over 12 weeks (Safren CBT, Solanto organisational CBT), not fast reward loops. Delay-aversion research supports immediate feedback, but no study tests reward-loop pacing in adult ADHD apps.

### Cited Findings
- **[MA, review of reviews]** BMC Psychiatry (2025) reviewed 26 reviews of digital ADHD interventions, most covering children and adolescents. Evidence was judged generally low quality and effectiveness "remains inconclusive", and 8/26 reviews (30.1%) reported adverse effects. — [BMC Psychiatry 2025](https://doaj.org/article/789ef3c13fd143468097858bb49f61e5)
- **[Pilot/Open]** Inflow CBT app, open study of 240 adults: usability assessments were completed by n=114 at 2 weeks, n=97 at 4 weeks and n=95 at 7 weeks, about a 60% drop from enrolment. — [PLOS Digital Health 2022](https://doaj.org/article/c137fe79dbed4a7b9b68cbc8fa175257)
- **[RCT, small]** FOCUS ADHD app (Brazil, n=73 adults, 3 months, 3 arms: TAU / TAU+app / TAU+app+medication discount): there was no between-group difference in medication adherence (medication possession ratio). The discount arm logged more early on and reached 100% app adoption. Weekly research-assistant calls may have inflated adherence in every arm. — [USP dissertation](https://www.teses.usp.br/teses/disponiveis/5/5142/tde-04082023-161602/?&lang=en); [PMC10377453](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10377453)
- **[RCT, small]** Smartphone-assisted vs brochure-assisted group psychoeducation (n=60 adults): the app arm improved inattention and impulsivity more and had higher homework compliance. Because the app supplemented a human-led group, this says little about how a standalone app retains users. — [hs-flensburg listing](https://hs-flensburg.de/publikationen/journal-article/1026)
- **[Pilot/Quasi-exp]** Numo app, 90 days: users were grouped by usage after the fact rather than randomized, and a gift certificate at day 90 was used "as motivation to maintain engagement". The engagement data are therefore confounded. — [Numo study summary](https://www.numo.ai/journal/numo-adhd-clinical-study-results)
- **[RCT]** Solanto et al. 2010, *Am J Psychiatry* 167:958–968. n=88 adults were randomized to a 12-week group CBT focused on time management, organisation and planning, or to supportive psychotherapy. The CBT arm did better on blind-evaluator, self-report and collateral ratings (OR=5.41, 95% CI 1.77–16.55). Response (≥30% drop in inattention) was 53% vs 28%. More home exercises completed went with greater improvement, but this association was observational. — [AJP](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2010.10101467); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/03/100330142437.htm)
- **[RCT]** Safren et al. 2010, *JAMA* 304. n=86 medication-treated adults with persistent symptoms received 12 individual sessions of CBT or of relaxation with educational support. 79 completed treatment and 70 completed 12-month follow-up. Module 1 covered organising and planning: a calendar plus a single task-list system, and breaking overwhelming tasks into steps. CBT produced lower post-treatment symptom and CGI scores, and the effect was reported as clinically important. I could not retrieve the exact effect sizes. — [MedicalNewsToday](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/198809/); [GlobalFamilyDoctor summary](https://www.globalfamilydoctor.com/Resources/A-Ztopiclisting/JournalWatch/CognitivebehavioraltherapyfortreatmentofadultADHD.aspx)
- **[Experimental, small]** In 55 undergraduates, hyperactive/impulsive symptoms predicted steep delay discounting only when rewards and delays were real, not hypothetical. — [PMC2788754](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2788754/)
- **[Experimental, children]** A 2025 study found delay aversion in ADHD spans a continuum, from decreased to increased discounting. It is not uniformly steeper. — [PMC11971471](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11971471)

### Inferences
- The best-evidenced adult ADHD interventions supply external structure: one calendar, one task list, tasks broken into steps, and a weekly cadence over 12 weeks. This supports **routine-anchored pacing with small, immediate feedback**. It does not support a dense loop of many parallel daily mechanics (lesson + check-in + tasks + coins + pet).
- Delay aversion supports making feedback **immediate**, for example a visible tick or reward at the moment of completion. It does not follow that there must be **many** rewards or a **fast cadence** of new content. These are separate design choices, and the evidence only speaks to the first.
- Heterogeneity in reward processing suggests one fixed reward intensity will not suit all ADHD users.

### Gaps
- I found no RCT that compares a gamified and a non-gamified app in **adults with ADHD**, and no pooled attrition figure for adult ADHD app trials.
- I found no study testing novelty-seeking as a driver of app churn in adult ADHD. The common claim that "ADHD users get bored and need novelty" appears to be practitioner opinion.
- I could not retrieve Safren 2010's exact effect sizes or the European Psychiatry digital-CBT-for-ADHD review's adherence results.

## Burnout/depression: low energy, task load, behavioral activation, and self-compassion vs streak/shame

### Takeaway
Real-world retention of mental-health apps is very low: median 3.3% at day 30. In a depression-app meta-analysis, gamification did not predict adherence, but intervention duration did. Behavioral activation apps that use small, measurable activities have RCT support. No study I found directly tests task load or streak/shame mechanics in burnt-out users.

### Cited Findings
- **[Obs, large usage panel]** Baumel et al. 2019, *JMIR* 21(9):e14567. Android mental-health apps with ≥10,000 installs had a median 15-day retention of 3.9% and 30-day retention of 3.3% (IQR 6.2%). Breathing-exercise apps had a median day-30 retention of 0.0%. Peer-support, mindfulness and tracker apps retained better. — [JMIR](https://www.jmir.org/2019/9/E14567/); [PMC6785720](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6785720)
- **[MA]** Six et al. 2021, *JMIR Mental Health*: 38 studies, 50 comparisons, n=8,110. Depression apps beat controls with g=−0.27 (95% CI −0.36 to −0.17), heterogeneity I²=0.76. In the meta-regression, gamification predicted neither adherence (β=−1.93, P=.40) nor symptom outcomes, while intervention duration predicted adherence (β=11.33, P=.003). The paper has internal inconsistencies, for example "nearly half" vs 71% (27/38) of studies being gamified. — [PMC8669581](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8669581/)
- **[RCT, large]** Moodivate behavioral activation app, *JAMA Internal Medicine* 2025: n=649 primary-care adults with at least moderate depression were randomized to the app or usual care for 12 weeks, and the app reduced depressive symptoms. Users set goals by life area with measurable activities, and the app includes badges. I did not confirm the effect sizes. — [Psychiatric News alert](https://alert.psychnews.org/2025/04/digital-behavioral-activation-app-shows.html)
- **[Pilot RCT]** In the earlier Moodivate pilot (n=52), about 70% were still using the app at 1 month and 50% at the end of the 8-week follow-up. — [PMC6582985](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6582985)
- **[RCT, small]** Maummove behavioral activation app (Korea, 2025): n=67 aged 20–30 with depressive symptoms, 8 weeks. I could not retrieve the outcomes. — [PMC12649501](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12649501/)
- **[Experimental, consumer research]** *Journal of Consumer Research* 2023, "Or Track? How Broken Streaks Affect Consumer Decisions". How a streak affects behaviour depends on how past behaviour is shown in the log, not on the behaviour itself. A broken streak lowers subsequent engagement, and this is amplified when people blame themselves for the break and attenuated when they can "repair" it. — [CU Boulder](https://www.colorado.edu/business/faculty-research/2023/04/19/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer-decisions); [INSEAD](https://www.insead.edu/faculty-research/publications/journal-articles/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer)
- **[Obs, small]** Lally et al. 2010, *Eur J Soc Psychol*. 96 volunteers each repeated one daily behaviour for 84 days. Median time to automaticity was about 66 days among the 39 participants with good curve fits, ranging from 18 to 254 days. The study has not been replicated. Secondary sources say a missed day did not materially derail habit formation, but I did not verify this against the paper. — [Univ. of Surrey interview](https://www.surrey.ac.uk/news/does-it-really-take-66-days-form-habit-we-asked-expert-dr-pippa-lally); [replication protocol](https://rr.peercommunityin.org/download/t_recommendations.reply_pdf.8382af6b73bdd0f7.6d616e757363726970745f7072657072696e742e706466.pdf)
- **[Unverified]** Several blogs cite a "2021 *Computers in Human Behavior*" study linking rigid streaks to guilt and abandonment. I could not locate the paper, so treat the claim as unverified. — [getfitcraft blog](https://getfitcraft.com/blog/streak-effect)

### Inferences
- With baseline 30-day retention around 3%, the first-weeks experience dominates outcomes. Users who drop do so early, which argues for a **low daily load** in week 1.
- Behavioral activation's evidence base supports small, measurable, self-chosen activities. Light badges appear in a successful RCT app (Moodivate), so light gamification is not incompatible with depression populations.
- The JCR streak findings give a mechanism for why streak breaks hurt self-blaming users: depressed and burnt-out users are prone to self-attribution. Streak repair or forgiveness and non-self-blaming copy should reduce that harm. This is an extrapolation from consumer samples, not clinical ones.
- Lally's slow, variable habit timeline, with missed days tolerated, supports routine anchoring over perfect-day mechanics.

### Gaps
- I found no study on **task count or daily load** and adherence in burnout or depression app users.
- I found no RCT of **self-compassion framing vs streak framing** in an app.
- I found no study of **burnout specifically**, as distinct from depression, and engagement with self-care apps.
- I did not retrieve graded task assignment trials that isolate "start small" as an active ingredient.

## Gamification: meta-analyses, novelty decay, streaks, virtual pets, points/coins and over-justification

### Takeaway
Gamification gives a small-to-medium short-term boost for physical activity, which roughly halves against active non-gamified controls and shrinks to very small about 14 weeks after the intervention ends. For depression apps it adds nothing measurable to adherence or outcomes. Novelty decays from about week 4, with partial recovery in one study. Tangible, completion-contingent rewards reliably undermine intrinsic motivation in lab studies. No controlled evidence exists for virtual-pet self-care apps like Finch.

### Cited Findings
- **[MA of RCTs]** Mazeas et al. 2022, *JMIR* 24(1):e26779: 16 RCTs, n=2,407.
  - Effect sizes: the overall effect on physical activity was g=0.42 (95% CI 0.14–0.69). Against active non-gamified PA interventions it was g=0.23 (0.05–0.41), and against waitlist g=0.58 (0.08–1.07).
  - Long-term: at a mean of about 14 weeks post-intervention, g=0.15 (0.07–0.23) in a sensitivity analysis. The unadjusted follow-up result was not significant (g=0.09).
  - Steps and MVPA: steps rose by about 1,610/day (95% CI 372–2,847), and there was no significant effect on MVPA.
  - The authors' interpretation: they argue the effect is not purely novelty, but concede it declines and that gamification can't be isolated from other intervention components.
  — [JMIR](https://www.jmir.org/2022/1/E26779/); [PMC8767479](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8767479/)
- **[MA]** Six et al. 2021 (depression apps, n=8,110): gamification was not a significant moderator of either outcome or adherence. — [PMC8669581](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8669581/)
- **[Systematic review]** Cheng et al. 2019, *JMIR Mental Health* 6(6):e13717. The literature on gamification in mental-health apps is sparse, its terms are inconsistent, and elements are often not grounded in behaviour-change theory. Gamification has been criticised for relying on extrinsic rewards. — [PMC6617915](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6617915/)
- **[Longitudinal, education]** Rodrigues et al. 2022: n=756 STEM students tracked at 7 time points over 14 weeks. The gamification effect began declining after about 4 weeks and kept falling for 2–6 weeks, then trended back up between weeks 6 and 10, which the authors call a "familiarization effect". The sample was students, not a health population. — [Durham repository](https://dro.dur.ac.uk/36081); [summary](https://www.universityxp.com/research/2022/10/3/gamification-suffers-from-the-novelty-effect-but-benefits-from-the-familiarization-effect-findings-from-a-longitudinal-study)
- **[Secondary]** Hamari et al. 2014 and Koivisto & Hamari 2014 are cited as finding that perceived benefits of a gamified service fall with time in use. I did not retrieve the originals. — [Rodrigues et al. via Durham](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1203232)
- **[MA, lab experiments]** Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999, *Psychological Bulletin* 125(6):627–668, 128 studies.
  - Free-choice intrinsic motivation was undermined by engagement-contingent (d=−0.40), completion-contingent (d=−0.36) and performance-contingent (d=−0.28) tangible rewards.
  - Self-reported interest fell by d=−0.15 (engagement-contingent) and d=−0.17 (completion-contingent).
  - Coins awarded for completing a task are completion-contingent rewards.
  — [PDF](https://home.ubalt.edu/ntygmitc/642/Articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf)
- **[Counterpoint, MA]** Cameron and colleagues dispute the size of the undermining effect in education settings, and Deci et al. 2001 responded. I did not extract numbers. — [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 2001](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2001_DeciKoestnerRyan.pdf); [RER 2001](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543071001001)
- **[Qual, autoethnography]** Zhou & Zhu, NordiCHI 2026 adjunct: a longitudinal autoethnography of gamified virtual-pet self-care apps. It describes a shift from "meaningful self-care support" to "reward-driven engagement and technology addiction". It is a single-author lived account, not a trial, and the abstract may not name Finch. — [Chalmers research](https://research.chalmers.se/en/publication/552903)
- **[Opinion/press]** Reviews of Finch say the pet is the hook that motivates activities. There are no outcome data, and the circulating retention comparisons from aggregator sites are unsourced. — [Paste](https://www.pastemagazine.com/article/finch-app-mental-health-virtual-pet-self-care); [YourStory](https://yourstory.com/2022/06/app-review-self-care-pet-finch-gamifies-mental-wellbeing)

### Inferences
- The best available estimate of what gamification buys is **modest and front-loaded**. It works against waitlist but adds only about g=0.2 over a well-designed non-gamified program, and it fades after the program ends. For depression apps it adds roughly nothing to adherence.
- Coins tied to task completion are exactly the reward type the over-justification literature flags. The risk is greatest for activities meant to become intrinsically valued, such as self-care and lessons. An informational frame ("you did X") is safer than a contingent payment frame, but I found no app trial confirming this.
- Novelty decline around week 4 lines up with the end of a typical trial window. A fast reward loop that peaks in weeks 1–3 may be judged early on metrics that later regress.
- The evidence does not show that gamification **hurts** engagement on average (null in Six 2021). The harm case rests on over-justification lab data, streak-break consumer experiments, and one qualitative pet-app account.

### Gaps
- I found **no RCT of a virtual-pet or virtual-room self-care app** for depression, burnout or ADHD. The classic CHI pet study (Fish'n'Steps, Lin et al. 2006) did not surface in searches, so I report no numbers for it.
- I found no RCT that isolates **streaks** or **points/coins** in mental-health apps.
- I found no study comparing **fast daily reward loops with slower routine-anchored pacing** in any of these populations. This is the core decision, and it has no direct evidence. An A/B test on 7-day activation and 30-day retention, split by purchase status, would be the way to answer it.
- I could not verify the claimed 2021 *Computers in Human Behavior* streak-guilt study.
